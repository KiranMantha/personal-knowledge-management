There is a strange moment that happens when you spend enough time inside large JavaScript codebases.

You stop being impressed by cleverness.

The first few years of development train you to admire complexity. The developer who writes the most abstract hook feels senior. The engineer who compresses thirty lines into seven feels smart. The person using five TypeScript utility types in one generic suddenly looks like a wizard.

Then you open enough production systems and realise something uncomfortable:

The best JavaScript developers are usually the least flashy people in the room.

Their code does not scream for attention. It survives contact with reality.

Over the last year, I studied roughly fifty JavaScript and TypeScript codebases across startups, SaaS products, internal enterprise tools, open-source projects, and some genuinely terrifying legacy systems held together by caffeine and fear.
